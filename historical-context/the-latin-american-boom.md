# What happened

The Boom is the name for the moment in the 1960s when a group of Latin American novelists started selling in large numbers across Latin America, Spain, and then in translation. The main names are Julio Cortázar (*Rayuela*, 1963), Mario Vargas Llosa (*La ciudad y los perros*, 1963), Carlos Fuentes (*La muerte de Artemio Cruz*, 1962), and Gabriel García Márquez (*Cien años de soledad*, 1967). Much of the business side ran through Barcelona: the publisher Seix Barral (its Biblioteca Breve prize went to Vargas Llosa in 1962) and the literary agent Carmen Balcells, who represented most of these writers.

The writers built on earlier work by Jorge Luis Borges, Alejo Carpentier, Miguel Ángel Asturias, and Juan Rulfo, whose *Pedro Páramo* (1955) García Márquez said he knew nearly by heart. They shared an interest in formal experiment (broken chronology, many narrators, readers given a choice of order) and in the region's politics. The Cuban Revolution of 1959 first united them; the Padilla affair of 1971, when Cuba jailed the poet Heberto Padilla and forced a public self-criticism, split them. Vargas Llosa broke with Castro, García Márquez stayed close to him. In 1976 Vargas Llosa punched García Márquez at a film screening in Mexico City, and the two did not speak again.

Cortázar (1914–1984) was born in Brussels to Argentine parents, grew up near Buenos Aires, and moved to Paris in 1951, partly out of dislike for Perón's government. He worked there as a UNESCO translator. Vargas Llosa (1936–2025) won the Nobel Prize in 2010; in 1990 he ran for president of Peru and lost to Alberto Fujimori. He died in Lima in April 2025.

# Grounds these books

- [[one-hundred-years-of-solitude]]: the book that made the Boom a mass phenomenon (1967)
- [[rayuela]]: Cortázar's experimental novel (1963), often named with *Cien años* as the movement's defining work
- [[bestiario]]: Cortázar's first story collection (1951), from before the Boom, already showing the fantastic-in-the-everyday method
- [[conversation-in-the-cathedral]]: Vargas Llosa's most structurally ambitious Boom novel (1969)
- [[the-feast-of-the-goat]]: late Vargas Llosa (2000), returning to the dictator novel, a form many Boom writers tried
- [[pedro-paramo]]: precursor (1955); its fragmented, ghost-filled Comala shaped García Márquez's Macondo
- [[el-llano-en-llamas]]: Rulfo's story collection (1953), the other half of his small, influential body of work
