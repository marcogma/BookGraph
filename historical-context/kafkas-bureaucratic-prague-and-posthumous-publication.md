# What happened

From 1908 until poor health forced his retirement in 1922, Kafka worked as a claims official at the Worker's Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia in Prague, assessing industrial accident claims inside the sprawling, multilingual bureaucracy of the late Austro-Hungarian Empire — by all accounts he was good at the job and respected there, which makes the bureaucratic nightmare of his fiction less an invention than an exaggeration of something he watched operate daily. He wrote at night after full workdays, was privately convinced most of his fiction was failed or unfinished, and before his death from tuberculosis in 1924 asked his close friend Max Brod to burn everything unpublished. Brod refused, and instead edited and published *The Trial* (1925) and *The Castle* (1926) — both left unfinished by Kafka, both structured by Brod's own editorial choices about ordering incomplete chapters — making Kafka's entire posthumous reputation the result of a promise his closest friend chose to break.

# Grounds these books

- [[the-trial]] (written 1914–15, published 1925) — Josef K.'s trial-by-paperwork is drawn directly from the claims-and-procedure world of Kafka's day job; the novel's own unfinished, out-of-order chapters are Brod's arrangement, not a form Kafka approved
- [[the-castle]] (written 1922, published 1926) — written just after Kafka's retirement, breaks off mid-sentence, unfinished at his death — its subject, a bureaucracy that withholds rather than judges, is the mirror image of the institutional world *The Trial* depicts
- [[the-metamorphosis]] (written 1912, published 1915): one of the few works Kafka published in his lifetime, written while he was working at the insurance institute
- [[letter-to-his-father]] (written 1919, published 1952): never delivered; Brod published it after Kafka's death along with the rest of the papers he was asked to burn
