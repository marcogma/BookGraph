# Summary

A short novel narrated by an unnamed young dreamer, lonely and isolated in St. Petersburg, who meets a young woman, Nastenka, crying on a bridge one night. Over four nights they grow close as she tells him about her love for a lodger who once lived with her grandmother and promised to return for her; the narrator falls in love with her himself even as he helps her wait for the other man. On the fourth night, just as Nastenka seems to be turning toward the narrator, the lodger reappears and she runs to him instead — though she writes the narrator a warm letter afterward, and he reflects that even this brief taste of connection was worth a lifetime of solitude.

# Main characters

- **The Dreamer** — the unnamed narrator, lonely, imaginative, prone to living inside stories of his own making
- **Nastenka** — the young woman he falls for, waiting for another man's return
- **The lodger** — Nastenka's earlier love, who eventually returns for her
- **The grandmother** — Nastenka's guardian, who pins her dress to her own to keep her from wandering

# Notes

The narrator is the main character, written in first person, but at points he narrates himself almost in the third person, as if he's very influenced by literature — telling his own life the way a novel would tell it. That habit isn't just a stylistic quirk, it's the way he learned to be through loneliness. It's not something he can turn off — even when Nastenka asks him directly to be less poetical, he says he doesn't know how.

The title itself is a metaphor. White nights are nights where you can't sleep — usually because of something occupying your mind: stress, love. The narrator lives a dream while staying awake, so the name fits perfectly. When Nastenka leaves, it's like the end of that dream he'd always wanted. He even says at the end that it was just a few minutes of bliss in a whole man's life — but the way he says it sounds like it was worth it to have lived that dream at all.

The relationship starts as strangers meeting through trouble — Nastenka is upset, crying, followed by another man, and the narrator helps her home. It slowly develops into something like love on his side, since he isn't used to speaking to anyone except in his own dreams, so she becomes the fulfillment of those dreams. On her side, she's in love with someone else and needs help, so it develops more like a brotherly relationship for her, even though she admits he has all the qualities she should fall in love with. When she realizes the lodger she's waiting for won't come, she accepts that she might learn to love the narrator instead — but when he finally does arrive, she goes straight to him, showing she never really intended otherwise.

I think Nastenka is being honest with him rather than cruel, technically — she tells him directly not to fall in love with her, even if she doesn't explain why. So even though the narrator was completely open about his feelings, it feels like she took advantage of that anyway, being coquettish at times. Honesty doesn't excuse not considering the effect on the other person, and that's what makes it feel cruel despite the honesty. And yet, even with that cruelty, the narrator seems happy just to have had those nights of company and to have lived his dream — even knowing it becomes a life of pain afterward.

Comparing him to the Underground Man: both reject fitting in, but the Underground Man is full of contradiction — proud and firm about being different, looking down on others as not enough, and then, in real situations, wanting exactly what he claims to despise: attention from friends he says he doesn't like. He had a real chance to deepen the relationship with Liza and sabotaged it out of his own insecurity. The White Nights narrator, by contrast, seems sure and proud of his feelings. He's always honest, and he doesn't retreat — he embraces his nature instead of undermining it at the last second.

On the Camus comparison: Sisyphus was honest about his own nature — refusing the gods' rules — and paid for it with eternal punishment. I think it's the same thing here. The narrator was honest about his feelings instead of playing society's games, and he "pays" for that with a lonely life. But both of them are free in their clarity, which is the core of Camus' challenge: can you still say yes to life knowing there's no ultimate reward? The narrator does. So does Sisyphus.
