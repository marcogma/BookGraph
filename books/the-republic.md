# Summary

A dialogue in ten books, narrated by Socrates, on the question "What is justice, and is the just person happier than the unjust?" In Book I Socrates refutes several definitions, including Thrasymachus's claim that justice is "the advantage of the stronger". Glaucon and Adeimantus then challenge him with the myth of the Ring of Gyges: would anyone stay just if they could become invisible? To find justice in the soul, Socrates first builds an ideal city (Kallipolis) with three classes: producers, guardians (soldiers), and rulers. The city is just when each class does its own job, and a soul is just when reason, spirit, and appetite are in the same order. The city requires censorship of poets, shared property and families among the guardians, equal education for women guardians, and a "noble lie" about the classes. The rulers must be philosophers. Books VI–VII give the analogy of the sun, the divided line, and the allegory of the cave, where prisoners take shadows for reality. Books VIII–IX describe how cities and souls decline: timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and tyranny, and argue the tyrant is the most miserable man. Book X banishes imitative poetry and ends with the Myth of Er, about the soul's choice of its next life.

# Main characters

- **Socrates**: the narrator and main speaker
- **Glaucon and Adeimantus**: Plato's older brothers, who push Socrates to defend justice
- **Thrasymachus**: a sophist who argues justice is the interest of the stronger
- **Cephalus and Polemarchus**: the old man at whose house they meet, and his son
- **Philosopher-kings**: rulers who know the Form of the Good
- **The allegory of the cave**: prisoners who mistake shadows for reality
- **The Ring of Gyges**: a ring that makes its wearer invisible, used to test whether justice is only fear of punishment

# Historical & cultural context

See [[fifth-century-athens]]. The dialogue is set around 411 BCE in the Piraeus, Athens' port, during the Peloponnesian War, and was written around 375 BCE. Several of the people in it were later caught up in the war's politics: Polemarchus was executed by the Thirty Tyrants.
