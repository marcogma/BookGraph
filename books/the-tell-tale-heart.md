# Summary

A selection of Poe's tales, poems, and essays; contents vary by edition, commonly including "The Black Cat", "The Cask of Amontillado", "The Raven", and "The Philosophy of Composition". In "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843), an unnamed narrator insists he is not mad while explaining how he killed an old man he lived with, because of the old man's pale blue "vulture eye". Each night for a week he opens the old man's door silently; on the eighth night he kills him and hides the dismembered body under the floorboards. Police come because of a neighbor's report of a scream. The narrator calmly shows them around and places his chair over the spot. He begins to hear a beating heart, louder and louder, and is sure the police hear it too and are mocking him, and he finally screams that they should tear up the planks. In "The Black Cat" a drunk kills his wife and walls up her body, and accidentally walls in the cat too, whose cry gives him away. In "The Cask of Amontillado" Montresor lures Fortunato into his wine cellar during carnival and walls him up alive.

# Main characters

- **The narrator**: the murderer who insists he is sane
- **The old man**: the victim with the 'vulture eye'
- **Montresor and Fortunato**: killer and victim in "The Cask of Amontillado"

# Historical & cultural context

See [[poe-and-the-invention-of-detective-fiction]]. "The Tell-Tale Heart" appeared in *The Pioneer* in January 1843. In "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) Poe claims he wrote "The Raven" step by step like solving a math problem; most critics think the essay is at least partly a joke.
