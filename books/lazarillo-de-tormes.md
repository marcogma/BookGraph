# Summary

Lázaro tells his own life in a long letter to "Vuestra Merced", an unnamed superior who has asked about a "case". Born by the river Tormes near Salamanca, son of a thief, he is handed by his mother to a blind beggar. The blind man beats him and teaches him to be sharp; Lázaro takes revenge by making him jump into a stone post. Next he serves a stingy priest at Maqueda who nearly starves him, and Lázaro steals bread from a locked chest with a copied key. Then he serves a poor squire in Toledo who has nothing to eat but keeps up the appearance of honor, and Lázaro ends up begging to feed him; the squire runs off to avoid his landlord. After shorter episodes with a friar, a seller of fake papal indulgences (buldero), a chaplain, and a constable, Lázaro gets a government post as town crier in Toledo and marries the servant of the Archpriest of San Salvador. Everyone says the wife is the archpriest's mistress. That is the "case": Lázaro accepts the arrangement and ends saying he was at that time "en la cumbre de toda buena fortuna".

# Main characters

- **Lázaro**: the narrator, who goes from orphan to town crier
- **The blind man**: his first and harshest master
- **The priest of Maqueda**: the miser who starves him
- **The squire (escudero)**: the penniless gentleman obsessed with honor
- **The buldero**: a seller of fake indulgences
- **The Archpriest of San Salvador**: Lázaro's last protector, his wife's lover

# Historical & cultural context

See [[the-spanish-golden-age-and-the-picaresque]]. Three editions appeared in 1554 (Burgos, Antwerp, Alcalá de Henares), and the author is still unknown; the anonymity is usually explained by the book's attacks on the clergy. It is the first picaresque novel, followed by Mateo Alemán's *Guzmán de Alfarache* (1599) and Quevedo's *El Buscón* (1626).
