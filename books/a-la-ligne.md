# Notes

The book follows a temp factory worker — fish canneries and slaughterhouses in Brittany — describing the repeated gestures of assembly-line work day after day: the noise, the cold, the fatigue. At the same time it shows what saves him: his prior literary education, memories, affection, moments of beauty or imagination he manages to hold onto despite the wear of the work.

I found it genuinely political, though not in the sense of a pamphlet or direct militancy — it's political from the inside. It doesn't make speeches, it just shows the work itself: the shifts, the exhaustion, the body breaking down, the repeated gestures. Shown with that much humanity, simply making it visible is already a political act — it exposes what's normally hidden, the life of the people who produce what we consume. It's a real critique of precarity and temporary contracts, of the worker becoming a replaceable part, but it also dignifies the worker rather than pitying him: no self-pity, no victimhood — the narrator keeps his culture, his humor, his poetry intact even inside the alienation.

The connection to Camus' *Myth of Sisyphus* is direct once I saw it. Camus' man is condemned to push a boulder forever, knowing it will fall again; the factory worker repeats the same gestures on the line — cut, pack, clean, start again. It's the same absurd gesture: repetition without apparent sense, effort with no real payoff. And what defines the absurd man for Camus isn't the work itself but being conscious of it. The narrator here knows his work is repetitive, alienating, going nowhere — and stays lucid anyway. That lucidity doesn't destroy him, it's what saves him: he keeps living, thinking, observing, finding poetry in the act of resisting, composing verses in his head on the line, reciting songs, remembering his wife, remembering poets. Camus is metaphysical about this; here it's sensory and embodied — you can smell the fish, feel the cold, hear the machines.
