# Summary

Candide is brought up in a Westphalian castle by the tutor Pangloss, who teaches that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds". He is thrown out for kissing the baron's daughter, Cunégonde. He is forced into the Bulgar army, sees war, survives a shipwreck and the Lisbon earthquake, and watches Pangloss hanged by the Inquisition. He finds Cunégonde again in Lisbon, kills two of her owners, and flees to South America, where he visits Paraguay's Jesuits and the hidden kingdom of El Dorado, full of gold. He loses most of the gold, travels with the pessimist Martin, and is cheated in Paris and Venice. At the end, in Turkey, he buys back Cunégonde, now ugly, and marries her anyway; Pangloss turns out to have survived. A Turkish farmer tells them that work keeps away boredom, vice and need, and Candide closes the book saying "il faut cultiver notre jardin".

# Main characters

- **Candide**: the naive young hero
- **Pangloss**: his tutor, the optimist philosopher
- **Cunégonde**: the baron's daughter he loves
- **The old woman**: Cunégonde's servant, daughter of a pope, who has suffered worse than all of them
- **Cacambo**: Candide's resourceful valet
- **Martin**: a pessimist scholar
- **Jacques**: a kind Anabaptist who drowns at Lisbon

# Historical & cultural context

Voltaire (François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778) published *Candide* anonymously in Geneva in 1759; it was banned in Paris and Geneva and sold widely anyway. Pangloss parodies the philosophy of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (and Alexander Pope's line "Whatever is, is right"). The Lisbon earthquake of November 1, 1755, which killed tens of thousands, had already led Voltaire to write a poem against this optimism. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) was going on as he wrote.
