# Summary

Camus' philosophical essay opens by declaring suicide the one truly serious philosophical problem, given that life appears to have no ultimate, rational meaning. Rather than answering that with despair or a religious "leap" into faith or transcendence (which he calls philosophical suicide), Camus argues for living fully and lucidly within the absurd — the unresolvable tension between humanity's demand for meaning and the universe's silence — without ever pretending the tension is resolved. He illustrates this "absurd" way of living through figures like Don Juan, the actor, and the conqueror, and closes with the image of Sisyphus, condemned by the gods to push a boulder uphill forever only to watch it roll back down, whom Camus imagines as happy — precisely because he embraces his fate with full clarity and no illusions.

# Notes

Camus says the one truly serious philosophical question is whether life is worth living — whether the absence of ultimate meaning should logically push us to suicide. I initially read this as "there is no truth," but it's more precise than that: he's not denying meaning exists so much as saying we can't find ultimate meaning in the universe, and yet we crave it anyway. That gap between the demand for clarity and the world's silence is what he calls the absurd — and importantly, the absurd isn't in me, and it isn't in the world, it's in the relationship between the two.

I first thought Camus was accusing dishonest philosophers of not really believing their own ideas — that if they did, they'd have already killed themselves. That's not quite it either. His actual target is subtler: philosophers who correctly diagnose the absurd and then "leap" out of it by smuggling meaning back in through religious faith or metaphysical hope (Kierkegaard, Chestov). He calls this philosophical suicide — a betrayal of the absurd's own logic, since they refuse to live inside the consequence of what they themselves discovered.

On "les murs" (the walls): I read them as our inability to reach certainty about anything — even seeing the same person a hundred times doesn't let you fully know them, and the only real certainty is nothingness, though we keep searching for meaning anyway. Camus' actual list of "absurd experiences" is more specific: weariness (routine suddenly feeling hollow), the passage of time (aging toward death we can't stop or understand), the strangeness of the world (things suddenly unfamiliar), and the silence of the world (we ask "why," and get no answer at all).

The first reaction to becoming aware of the absurd is emptiness. Religions and philosophies try to fill that emptiness with hope beyond our actual capacities — Camus calls this "the leap." Instead, he thinks we should keep living inside the absurd and still find it valuable. The universe is indifferent to our choices and aspirations; that silence is the heart of the absurd, because it shows life can feel contradictory or empty. But realizing this is also what lets us give our own sense to how we want to live, instead of chasing a goal indifferent to us. Finding the sense is on us, not the universe.

Camus rejects being called an existentialist, and the difference isn't really about believing meaning has to come from the individual rather than the world — most existentialists believe that too. The difference is in what happens next. Kierkegaard leaps to faith in God, Jaspers to transcendence, Sartre (even atheist) to a kind of self-invented meaning that functions as a substitute for God. Camus calls all of this "le saut" — escaping the absurd by importing hope from outside it. His absurd hero stops at the absurd, stays lucid, and refuses every consolation. He doesn't create meaning in the sense of building a new absolute; he endures meaninglessness and lives passionately anyway.

Don Juan, in the "Le donjuanisme" chapter, is Camus' clearest absurd hero of love: he genuinely stops being interested and moves on, not because he wants more women but because interest is temporal and there's no reason to stay once it's gone. He doesn't follow a religious or moral rule about love — he accepts his own nature and is happy in it. That's the key: he isn't a collector accumulating conquests, he's faithful to each moment of passion without being faithful to the idea of permanence. He never seeks "the one," never lies to himself about forever.

Faust, mentioned as a contrast, fails exactly where Don Juan succeeds — he sells his soul because he wants more, refusing to accept the finitude of a single life. That's not living the moment, it's living for something beyond it, and that refusal to accept limits — his belief that not having everything means he'll be miserable — is precisely the metaphysical leap the absurd hero has to reject.

I was also reading philosophy of science alongside this, which raised a real question for me: is searching for ultimate truth against the absurd? I think the answer is that it depends on the motive. If science becomes a religion — a hope that it will one day explain everything and hand back meaning — then yes, that's a scientific version of Faust. But curiosity itself isn't against the absurd. A scientist can accept we'll never know everything and still pursue understanding out of curiosity and rebellion rather than despair or hope for salvation — which is exactly Sisyphus pushing the stone with full clarity and no illusions.

# Historical & cultural context

Published in 1942, written in Nazi-occupied France, the same year as Camus' novel *The Stranger* — he became active in the French Resistance shortly after, editing the underground (later overground) newspaper *Combat*. The essay's insistence on living lucidly inside an unresolved, meaningless situation without either despair or false hope reads very differently against that immediate context — not as an abstract thought experiment, but as a philosophy written by someone living inside an actual occupation with no guaranteed ending.
