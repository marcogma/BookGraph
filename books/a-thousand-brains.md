# Summary

Hawkins argues that the neocortex, about 70 percent of the human brain, is made of around 150,000 cortical columns that all run the same basic algorithm. Each column learns complete models of objects and concepts using "reference frames", similar to the grid cells that animals use to map places. So the brain doesn't have one model of a coffee cup but thousands, built from different sensory inputs, which reach a consensus by voting: the "Thousand Brains Theory". Thinking, on this view, is a kind of movement through reference frames, and knowledge of abstract things like mathematics or language is organized the same way. Part 2 argues that current AI is not intelligent because it lacks such models, and that intelligent machines will need to learn the same way; he does not think they pose an existential threat on their own. Part 3 discusses threats to humanity coming from the old, emotional parts of the brain and false beliefs, and proposes ways to preserve human knowledge for the future, even if humanity dies out.

# Main characters

- **Neocortex**: the outer layer of the brain responsible for intelligence
- **Cortical column**: the repeating unit of the neocortex
- **Reference frames**: the structure each column uses to model the world
- **Grid cells**: neurons that map location, which Hawkins thinks the cortex reuses
- **Vernon Mountcastle**: the neuroscientist who first proposed that columns share one algorithm

# Historical & cultural context

Hawkins (born 1957) invented the PalmPilot and co-founded Palm and Handspring before turning to neuroscience. He founded the Redwood Neuroscience Institute in 2002 and the company Numenta in 2005. The book (2021) has a foreword by Richard Dawkins, the author of *The Selfish Gene*. His earlier book was *On Intelligence* (2004).
