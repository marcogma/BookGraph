# Summary

A novella in two parts: a bitter, contradictory philosophical monologue by an unnamed narrator (the "Underground Man") attacking rationalism and utopian visions of a perfectly ordered society, followed by a narrative from his younger years — an uninvited dinner with old schoolmates that humiliates him, and his subsequent encounter with a young prostitute, Liza, whom he first draws in with an impassioned speech about love and dignity, then cruelly rejects out of fear of real intimacy, leaving them both wounded.

# Main characters

- **The Underground Man** — the unnamed narrator, spiteful, self-aware, isolated by both choice and fear
- **Liza** — a young prostitute he draws close and then cruelly pushes away
- **Zverkov** — the former schoolmate whose farewell dinner the narrator forces his way into

# Notes

The narrator explains his own way of seeing life in the first chapter: sick, egocentric, mean, full of contradictions — he claims a huge love for himself while also considering himself the worst of men. For him freedom has a cost. There are things we're supposed to accept as true — 2+2=4 — but forcing yourself to accept that as the whole of reality is itself a betrayal of freedom, which then has to be pursued even when it isn't logical. That pursuit can lead straight to pain, and I think that's the point: human dignity lies in unpredictability. A world with no room for irrationality is a world with no room for rebellion, so pain becomes meaningful because it's chosen — it affirms autonomy rather than just being suffered.

The dinner scene with his old schoolmates is where this gets concrete. He wants to go because part of him genuinely craves connection, even while he keeps insisting he hates all of them. He wants to be perceived, even knowing he isn't welcome — and that's actually more of an incentive, not less, because it guarantees discomfort for everyone, forces them to notice him, and lets him reaffirm his own hatred and misery. At the same time, not going would mean following the others' expectations instead of his own will, so going is also a way of proving to himself that he's in control of his decisions. It's self-sabotage and self-assertion at once.

With Liza it's the same pattern, deeper. He first sees her as a beautiful woman and, being lonely, craves her attention — but the only way he knows how to relate is through cruelty and bluntness, finding the worst in her and naming it. Then he flips and starts talking about love and what family love should look like. I think what he genuinely craves is that kind of love, but he also feels a kind of relief seeing others without it too — it confirms how miserable life is, and he's not alone in that. When he tells her everything and sees that his speech actually moves her, he feels special, different — chosen, almost. But that's too much for him. Before he can let himself pursue it, he sabotages it by being cruel and horrible again. The vulnerability of being genuinely seen and maybe loved disarms him more than anything else in the book.

Reading this through Nietzsche, the narrator is stuck in the lion stage of the three metamorphoses — he says "no" to inherited morality, to rationalism, to the utopian promise of a world governed purely by reason, because he sees it as a cage. But he never becomes the child; he never moves from rejection to creation. He just negates, endlessly, consumed by spite rather than vitality. He has none of the will to power that would let him turn suffering into strength — his will is inward and corrosive. It's the wrong kind of nihilism, the passive kind: believing in nothing, trusting no one, refusing to take responsibility for creating meaning. And despite all his talk of superiority and isolation, he desperately wants to be seen — that contradiction, proclaiming freedom while being enslaved by fear of connection, is exactly the kind of spiritual failure Nietzsche warns against.

Kierkegaard's frame fits too, even without having read him directly: despair isn't sadness, it's being alienated from your true self by refusing to become it. The narrator is stuck because he won't reconcile with who he is or commit to anything beyond himself — he hides behind intelligence, irony, and cruelty precisely because committing (what Kierkegaard calls the leap of faith) would mean risking humiliation or real transformation. He's trapped in the aesthetic stage: living for himself, using wit and cleverness to avoid responsibility, when he already half-knows, in the Liza scene, that he should live more ethically. Self-awareness without the courage to act on it is the worst form of despair, and that's exactly him.

Compared to Raskolnikov, the contrast is total. Raskolnikov acts — he believes in and justifies a murderous idea, and then the guilt takes over him afterward. The Underground Man never risks anything; he thinks endlessly and never acts, so he never even gets the chance to be broken open by guilt the way Raskolnikov is, and never gets the chance at the kind of redemption Sonya offers Raskolnikov. Liza could have been that for him, but he refuses grace where Raskolnikov eventually moves toward it.

I also thought about Faust here, even though it might seem unrelated at first. Faust has all the knowledge he could want and still wants more, so he sells his soul to the devil — for earthly love, which he ends up regretting. That's its own kind of existential crisis: the restlessness of someone who has everything except the ability to be satisfied by it, and who tries to escape human limits through a shortcut rather than accepting them.

# Historical & cultural context

See [[dostoevskys-mock-execution-and-siberian-exile]] — written in 1864, the first major work after Dostoevsky's return from exile. Widely read as his direct rebuttal to Nikolai Chernyshevsky's utopian novel *What Is to Be Done?* (1863), which imagined a perfectly rational society engineered around enlightened self-interest — exactly the "crystal palace" the Underground Man rages against.
