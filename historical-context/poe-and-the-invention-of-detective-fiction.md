# What happened

Edgar Allan Poe was born in Boston in 1809 to traveling actors. His father left and his mother died when he was two, and he was raised in Richmond by the merchant John Allan, who never formally adopted him. He was thrown out of the University of Virginia over gambling debts and expelled from West Point. In 1836 he married his cousin Virginia Clemm, then thirteen; she died of tuberculosis in 1847. He tried to live from magazine writing and editing, which paid badly. "The Raven" (1845) made him famous but not rich. He died in Baltimore in October 1849 after being found delirious in the street, and the cause is still unknown. His rival Rufus Griswold wrote a hostile obituary and memoir that shaped his reputation as a drunk and madman for decades.

With "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841) Poe created the detective story: an eccentric amateur genius (C. Auguste Dupin), a narrator-friend who admires him, a locked-room puzzle, and a final explanation. Arthur Conan Doyle acknowledged the debt, and Holmes and Watson follow the Dupin model. Charles Baudelaire translated Poe into French in the 1850s, which made him central to French literature. Julio Cortázar translated his complete prose into Spanish (1956).
