# Notes

Dostoevsky always writes male protagonists, but the feminine figure is always central and drives the story somehow: Raskolnikov's fiancée in *Crime and Punishment*, the woman the narrator falls for in *White Nights*, the prostitute in *Notes from Underground*, Nastasia in *The Idiot*. But in *Karamazov* I couldn't place who the central female figure was.

Working through it, I realized there isn't one — there are two, and that split is the point. Grushenka is the more determinant one in terms of action: she's the center of the conflict between Dmitri and his father, representing desire and temptation but also an unexpected capacity for redemption — she actually evolves morally over the novel, unlike a lot of Dostoevsky's other women who stay more fixed. Katerina Ivanovna is the other pole, more psychological than passionate: pride, sacrifice, a kind of moral self-destruction, tied to Dmitri but in tension with Ivan. So where *Crime and Punishment* has Sonia as a clear moral axis and *The Idiot* has Nastasia concentrating the whole drama in one person, *Karamazov* divides that role in two. The "central feminine figure" stops being a person and becomes a tension between two types of love — passion/redemption on one side, pride/sacrifice on the other.

I also noticed I genuinely struggle to keep the characters' names straight across Dostoevsky's novels — there are just so many, and they all carry the same kind of weight. What helps is not memorizing names but pairing one trait with one relationship. Across the novels I've read, the pattern repeats: a man defined by a single dominant force (pride, passion, reason, cynicism, faith) orbiting a woman who represents either redemption or destruction — and Dostoevsky's structure is basically always "does the man accept the possibility of being saved, or destroy it."
