# Summary

A short treatise in twenty-six chapters on how a prince gains and keeps power. Machiavelli sorts principalities into hereditary and new ones, and new ones by how they were acquired: by one's own arms and *virtù* (ability, energy), by fortune and others' arms, by crime, or by the people's favor. He argues that a prince must rely on his own troops, not mercenaries or allies. Chapters 15–19 argue that a prince must learn "how not to be good" when necessary. It is safer to be feared than loved, if he can't be both, but he must avoid being hated. He should be like both the fox and the lion, and he must seem merciful, faithful, honest and religious while being ready to act otherwise. Fortune controls about half of what happens, and the bold can master the rest. The last chapter calls on the Medici to free Italy from foreign invaders.

# Main characters

- **Virtù**: the prince's ability and drive
- **Fortuna**: chance, which virtù can partly master
- **Cesare Borgia**: son of Pope Alexander VI, Machiavelli's main example of a new prince
- **Agathocles**: the tyrant of Syracuse who rose by crime
- **Lorenzo de' Medici, Duke of Urbino**: the dedicatee

# Historical & cultural context

Machiavelli (1469–1527) served the Florentine Republic for fourteen years as a diplomat and secretary, and met Cesare Borgia on missions. When the Medici returned to power in 1512, he lost his post, and in 1513 he was arrested on suspicion of conspiracy and tortured with the strappado. Released, he retired to his farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina and wrote *The Prince* that year, hoping for a job with the Medici. It circulated in manuscript and was printed in 1532, after his death. The Church put his works on the Index in 1559. His longer *Discourses on Livy* defend republics.
