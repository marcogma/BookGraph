# Summary

About 240 verse fables in twelve books, published in three collections between 1668 and 1694. Animals act like humans to show how the world works, and each fable has a short moral, often ironic. The best known include "La Cigale et la Fourmi" (the grasshopper sings all summer and the ant refuses to feed her in winter), "Le Corbeau et le Renard" (the fox flatters the crow into dropping his cheese), "Le Loup et l'Agneau" ("La raison du plus fort est toujours la meilleure"), "Le Lièvre et la Tortue" ("Rien ne sert de courir; il faut partir à point"), "La Grenouille qui se veut faire aussi grosse que le bœuf", "Le Chêne et le Roseau", and "Les Animaux malades de la peste", where the powerful animals confess terrible crimes and are excused while the donkey, who ate a little grass, is killed.

# Historical & cultural context

See [[moliere-and-the-court-of-louis-xiv]], which covers La Fontaine's link to Fouquet. The first six books (1668) adapt Aesop and the Roman fabulist Phaedrus; the later books draw on the Indian *Panchatantra*, known in France through a translation attributed to "Pilpay". The first collection was dedicated to the six-year-old Dauphin. Many morals are veiled comments on the court, where the lion stands for the king. French schoolchildren still learn several fables by heart.
