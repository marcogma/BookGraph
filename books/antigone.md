# Summary

After Oedipus's sons Eteocles and Polynices kill each other fighting over Thebes, their uncle Creon, now king, orders that Eteocles be buried with honor and Polynices, who attacked the city, be left unburied for the dogs and birds, under penalty of death. Antigone tells her sister Ismene she will bury him anyway, because the laws of the gods require it; Ismene refuses to help. Antigone covers the body with dust, is caught, and defends her act to Creon. He sentences her to be walled up alive in a cave. His son Haemon, engaged to Antigone, argues with him and leaves in anger. The blind prophet Tiresias warns Creon that the gods are angry. Creon gives in and goes first to bury Polynices and then to free Antigone, but she has already hanged herself. Haemon attacks his father, then kills himself on his sword over her body. Creon's wife Eurydice, hearing the news, kills herself too, cursing him. Creon is left alone and broken.

# Main characters

- **Antigone**: daughter of Oedipus, who buries her brother
- **Creon**: king of Thebes, who forbids it
- **Ismene**: Antigone's cautious sister
- **Haemon**: Creon's son, engaged to Antigone
- **Tiresias**: the blind prophet
- **Eurydice**: Creon's wife
- **Polynices and Eteocles**: the dead brothers

# Historical & cultural context

See [[fifth-century-athens]]. First staged around 441 BCE. Hegel, in his lectures on aesthetics, read the play as a collision of two legitimate claims, family and religious duty against the law of the state. Jean Anouilh's *Antigone* (1944), staged in occupied Paris, was read by audiences as being about resistance.
