# Summary

In St. Petersburg in the summer heat, Rodion Raskolnikov, a poor former law student, kills an old pawnbroker, Alyona Ivanovna, with an axe, convinced by his theory that extraordinary men like Napoleon have the right to step over moral law. Her half-sister Lizaveta walks in and he kills her too. He takes little and hides it under a stone. Afterward he falls into fever and paranoia. He meets the drunk former clerk Marmeladov, whose daughter Sonya became a prostitute to feed the family; when Marmeladov is run over by a carriage, Raskolnikov gives the family his money. The investigating magistrate Porfiry Petrovich suspects him and plays a psychological game with him. Raskolnikov's sister Dunya is pursued by the corrupt Luzhin and by the debauched Svidrigailov, who overhears Raskolnikov's confession to Sonya; after Dunya rejects him, Svidrigailov shoots himself. Sonya reads him the raising of Lazarus and urges him to confess. He confesses at the police station and is sentenced to eight years of hard labor in Siberia. Sonya follows him. In the epilogue, after an illness, he finally breaks down at her feet and begins to love her, and a new life begins.

# Main characters

- **Rodion Raskolnikov**: the student who commits the murders
- **Sonya Marmeladova**: the prostitute whose faith leads him to confess
- **Porfiry Petrovich**: the magistrate who plays with him
- **Dmitri Razumikhin**: his loyal friend
- **Avdotya (Dunya) Raskolnikova**: his sister
- **Pulcheria Alexandrovna**: his mother
- **Arkady Svidrigailov**: Dunya's former employer, a predator
- **Pyotr Luzhin**: Dunya's calculating fiancé
- **Semyon Marmeladov**: Sonya's drunken father
- **Katerina Ivanovna**: Marmeladov's consumptive wife
- **Alyona Ivanovna and Lizaveta**: the murdered pawnbroker and her half-sister

# Historical & cultural context

See [[dostoevskys-mock-execution-and-siberian-exile]]: the epilogue's Siberian prison comes from Dostoevsky's own years in Omsk. The novel was serialized in *The Russian Messenger* in 1866. Dostoevsky wrote it while deep in gambling debt. He was also bound by a contract with the publisher Stellovsky that would have given away the rights to his work if he missed a deadline. To meet it, he dictated *The Gambler* in twenty-six days to the stenographer Anna Snitkina, whom he married the next year.
