# What happened

On April 23, 1849, the 27-year-old Dostoevsky was arrested with other members of the Petrashevsky Circle, a group of young intellectuals who met to discuss forbidden books and utopian socialist ideas — nothing more organized than a reading and discussion circle, but treated by Tsar Nicholas I's government as a revolutionary conspiracy. On December 22, 1849, Dostoevsky and his fellow prisoners were taken to Semyonovsky Square in St. Petersburg, told they would die by firing squad, given last rites, and had the first three men tied to posts — before a messenger arrived with the tsar's staged, last-second reprieve, commuting the sentence to hard labor. It was deliberate theater, designed by Nicholas I to terrify. Dostoevsky served four years of katorga (hard labor) in an Omsk prison camp, shackled the entire time, followed by several more years of compulsory military exile in Semipalatinsk before he was allowed to return to European Russia around 1859.

# Grounds these books

- [[white-nights]] — written and published in 1848, months *before* the arrest: the sentimental, dreamy, apolitical Dostoevsky of before this event, useful as a baseline for how sharply his fiction changed afterward
- [[notes-from-underground]] (1864) — the first major work after his return, and the bitterness/spite of the Underground Man is widely read as shaped by the decade he'd just lived through
- [[the-idiot]] (1868–69) — Myshkin's epilepsy is Dostoevsky's own, and the condemned man's account of his last minutes (one of the novel's embedded "essays") is close to a first-hand transcription of Semyonovsky Square
- [[the-meek-one]] (1876) and [[dream-of-a-ridiculous-man]] (1877) — both late short pieces from his *Diary of a Writer*, written once his views had hardened into the religious/conservative position this period produced
- [[the-brothers-karamazov]] (1879–80, his final novel) — the religious wrestling running through Ivan and Alyosha traces back to the faith he leaned on through the camp years
- [[demons]] — written in response to a separate, later event (the 1869 Nechaev affair — see that book's own note), but the paranoid, radicalized political world it depicts is unimaginable without Dostoevsky's own youthful entanglement with, and later horror at, revolutionary politics
