# Summary

Dawkins argues that natural selection acts on genes, not on individuals or species. Long ago, replicating molecules appeared, and those that copied themselves best spread. Living bodies are "survival machines" built by genes to carry and copy them. A gene is "selfish" in the sense that it spreads if its effects help copies of itself survive, which can produce cooperative behavior in the bodies carrying it. Dawkins uses this view to explain kin selection (W. D. Hamilton's idea that helping relatives helps shared genes), parental care and conflict between parents and children, conflict between the sexes, reciprocal altruism (Robert Trivers), and evolutionarily stable strategies (John Maynard Smith), such as hawks and doves. In chapter 11 he coins the word "meme" for units of culture (tunes, ideas, fashions) that spread by imitation and evolve like genes. Later editions add "Nice Guys Finish First", on how cooperation can evolve in the repeated prisoner's dilemma, and "The Long Reach of the Gene", which previews his *The Extended Phenotype*.

# Main characters

- **Replicator**: anything that makes copies of itself
- **Survival machine**: an organism, as a vehicle for its genes
- **Kin selection**: helping relatives who share your genes
- **Evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)**: a behavior that can't be beaten by an alternative once common
- **Meme**: a unit of cultural transmission

# Historical & cultural context

Published in 1976. Dawkins (born 1941 in Nairobi) is a British biologist who taught at Oxford, where he held a professorship for the public understanding of science from 1995 to 2008. The book popularized ideas already developed by Hamilton, Trivers, Maynard Smith and George C. Williams. Dawkins later became known as an atheist writer with *The God Delusion* (2006). He wrote the foreword to Jeff Hawkins's *A Thousand Brains* (2021).
