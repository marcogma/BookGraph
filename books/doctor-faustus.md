# Summary

Faustus, a German scholar at Wittenberg who has mastered logic, medicine, law and theology, finds them all useless and turns to magic. He summons the devil Mephistopheles and signs a contract in his own blood with Lucifer: twenty-four years of service and knowledge, after which he gives up his soul. A Good Angel and a Bad Angel argue over him throughout. He gets less than he hoped: Mephistopheles won't tell him who made the world. Faustus spends his years on tricks and pranks: he makes himself invisible to box the Pope's ears, raises the ghost of Alexander the Great for the Emperor, and fetches grapes out of season for a duchess. He sees a parade of the Seven Deadly Sins. Near the end, he has Helen of Troy summoned ("Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?") and takes her as his lover. On his last night he tells the scholars the truth, and in his final speech, as the clock strikes, he begs for time and offers to burn his books. Devils carry him to hell.

# Main characters

- **Faustus**: the scholar who sells his soul
- **Mephistopheles**: the devil who serves him and admits he is himself in hell
- **Lucifer**: the prince of devils
- **The Good Angel and the Bad Angel**: the voices pulling Faustus each way
- **Wagner**: Faustus's servant
- **Valdes and Cornelius**: the magicians who teach him
- **Helen of Troy**: the spirit he takes as a lover

# Historical & cultural context

See [[elizabethan-theatre-marlowe-and-shakespeare]]. Marlowe's source was the *English Faust Book* (1592), a translation of the German *Historia von D. Johann Fausten* (1587), which was based on a real 16th-century traveling magician. The play survives in two different texts, the A-text (1604) and the longer B-text (1616). Goethe's *Faust* (1808, 1832) is the other major version of the story; in Goethe, Faust is saved.
