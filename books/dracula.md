# Summary

Told through diaries, letters and newspaper clippings. The young English solicitor Jonathan Harker travels to Transylvania to arrange the purchase of an English estate for Count Dracula, and realizes he is a prisoner in the castle, menaced by three vampire women. Dracula sails to England, where his ship runs aground at Whitby with the crew dead. In England he attacks Lucy Westenra, friend of Harker's fiancée Mina. Lucy's suitors (Dr. John Seward, who runs an asylum, Arthur Holmwood, and the American Quincey Morris) and Seward's teacher, the Dutch professor Abraham Van Helsing, give her blood transfusions, but she dies and becomes a vampire who preys on children. They drive a stake through her heart. Harker returns and marries Mina. The group finds Dracula's boxes of earth around London and sterilizes them. Dracula attacks Mina and makes her drink his blood, linking her mind to his. Using that link, they follow him as he flees back to Transylvania. At sunset outside his castle, Harker cuts his throat and Quincey stabs him in the heart, and he crumbles to dust. Quincey dies of his wounds. Seven years later the Harkers have a son named Quincey.

# Main characters

- **Count Dracula**: the vampire nobleman
- **Jonathan Harker**: the solicitor who visits his castle
- **Mina Murray (Harker)**: his fiancée, then wife
- **Lucy Westenra**: Mina's friend, Dracula's first English victim
- **Abraham Van Helsing**: the Dutch professor who knows what Dracula is
- **Dr. John Seward**: director of the asylum, Lucy's suitor
- **Arthur Holmwood (Lord Godalming)**: Lucy's fiancé
- **Quincey Morris**: a Texan, Lucy's other suitor
- **R. M. Renfield**: Seward's patient, who eats flies and serves Dracula

# Historical & cultural context

See [[victorian-fin-de-siecle-gothic]]. Published in 1897. Stoker never visited Transylvania and researched it in libraries, including at Whitby, where he found the name "Dracula" in a book on Wallachia. The historical Vlad III Dracula (Vlad the Impaler), 15th-century prince of Wallachia, gave the name, though how much of him is in the character is debated. F. W. Murnau's *Nosferatu* (1922) was an unauthorized adaptation, and Stoker's widow won a lawsuit ordering its copies destroyed.
