# What happened

In 1492 the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, took Granada and expelled Spain's Jews; Columbus reached America the same year. Jews who converted stayed on as *conversos*, watched by the Inquisition (founded 1478) and by "purity of blood" statutes. Over the next century Spain became the main European power and produced its greatest literature, the Siglo de Oro, roughly from Garcilaso and the 16th century to the death of Calderón in 1681. Spain also went bankrupt several times, and the gap between imperial glory and street poverty runs through its fiction.

*La Celestina* (1499), by the converso law student Fernando de Rojas, came at the opening of the period. The anonymous *Lazarillo de Tormes* (1554) started the picaresque: a first-person life of a poor boy (*pícaro*) serving a series of masters, with the clergy and the honor-obsessed gentry exposed along the way. The Inquisition put it on its Index of prohibited books in 1559, and an expurgated version circulated from 1573. Miguel de Cervantes (1547–1616) lost the use of his left hand at the Battle of Lepanto (1571), and spent five years as a captive in Algiers. He then worked as a tax collector, landing in jail more than once, before publishing the first part of *Don Quixote* in 1605. In 1614 a writer calling himself Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda published a fake second part, and Cervantes' real Part Two (1615) mocks it.

# Grounds these books

- [[la-celestina]]: 1499, by Fernando de Rojas, at the start of the period
- [[lazarillo-de-tormes]]: 1554; the first picaresque novel, banned in 1559
- [[don-quixote]]: 1605 and 1615; Cervantes' parody of chivalric romance
- [[exemplary-novels]]: 1613; Cervantes' twelve short novels, including two picaresque ones
