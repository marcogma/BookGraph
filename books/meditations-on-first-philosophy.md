# Summary

Six meditations, written as if over six days. In the first, Descartes resolves to doubt everything he can: the senses deceive, he might be dreaming, and an all-powerful evil demon might be deceiving him even about mathematics. In the second he finds one thing he can't doubt: while he is thinking, he exists ("I am, I exist"). He is a thinking thing. A piece of wax that changes all its sensory qualities when melted shows that bodies are known by the intellect, not the senses. In the third he argues that his idea of an infinite, perfect God could only have been caused by such a God, so God exists. The fourth explains error: God gave him a limited intellect and an unlimited will, and he errs when his will judges beyond what he clearly understands. The fifth gives a second proof of God (existence is part of the idea of a perfect being) and grounds mathematics. The sixth argues that the mind is distinct from the body, though closely joined to it, and that since God is not a deceiver, material things exist.

# Main characters

- **Methodical doubt**: rejecting anything that can be doubted, to find a firm foundation
- **The evil demon**: the hypothesis of a powerful deceiver
- **The cogito**: "I am, I exist", certain whenever I think it
- **Clear and distinct ideas**: Descartes' test for truth
- **Mind–body dualism**: mind as thinking substance, body as extended substance
- **The Cartesian circle**: Arnauld's objection that Descartes uses clear and distinct ideas to prove the God who guarantees them

# Historical & cultural context

See [[seventeenth-century-rationalism]]. The phrase "cogito, ergo sum" ("je pense, donc je suis") comes from the *Discourse on Method* (1637), not from the *Meditations*, which uses "I am, I exist". The book was printed with six sets of objections and Descartes' replies.
