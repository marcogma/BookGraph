# What happened

Realism in the French novel meant a turn away from Romanticism toward ordinary, often provincial and bourgeois life, described in exact detail without a moralizing narrator. Gustave Flaubert (1821–1880) was its central figure: *Madame Bovary* (1856) led to his prosecution for offending public morals in 1857, and he was acquitted. In the 1870s Émile Zola pushed further into naturalism, which treated characters as products of heredity and environment, like a scientific experiment.

Guy de Maupassant (1850–1893) was Flaubert's protégé. His mother, Laure Le Poittevin, was the sister of Flaubert's closest childhood friend, and Flaubert trained him for years, making him rewrite and observe. Maupassant fought in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, which ended in French defeat and the Prussian occupation of Normandy. In 1880 the Zola circle published *Les Soirées de Médan*, six stories about that war; Maupassant's "Boule de suif" was the standout, and Flaubert called it a masterpiece weeks before he died. Maupassant then published about 300 stories and six novels in ten years. He had syphilis, attempted suicide in 1892, and died in an asylum in Paris in 1893.
