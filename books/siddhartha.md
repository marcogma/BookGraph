# Summary

In ancient India, Siddhartha, the son of a Brahmin, leaves home with his friend Govinda to look for enlightenment. They live for years as ascetic samanas, then hear the Buddha, Gotama. Govinda becomes his follower; Siddhartha respects the Buddha but decides that wisdom cannot be taught and leaves to find his own way. In a city he meets the courtesan Kamala, who teaches him love, and he becomes rich working for the merchant Kamaswami. Over the years he sinks into gambling, drinking, and boredom. Disgusted, he leaves, nearly drowns himself in a river, and hears the sacred syllable Om. He stays with the ferryman Vasudeva and learns to listen to the river. Kamala, now a Buddhist pilgrim, comes with Siddhartha's son, is bitten by a snake and dies. The spoiled boy hates his father and runs away, and Siddhartha suffers as other parents do. Listening to the river, he finally reaches peace and understands the unity of all things. In old age Govinda visits him and, kissing his forehead, sees in his face all forms of life at once.

# Main characters

- **Siddhartha**: the seeker
- **Govinda**: his friend, who follows the Buddha
- **Gotama**: the Buddha
- **Kamala**: the courtesan who teaches him love
- **Kamaswami**: the merchant he works for
- **Vasudeva**: the ferryman who teaches him to listen to the river
- **Young Siddhartha**: his son by Kamala

# Historical & cultural context

See [[hesse-jungian-analysis-and-the-turn-east]]. Published in 1922. Hesse finished the first part quickly, then stopped for more than a year because, he said, he had to live through what Siddhartha learns before he could write it. The first part is dedicated to the French writer Romain Rolland, the second to Hesse's cousin Wilhelm Gundert, a scholar of Japan.
