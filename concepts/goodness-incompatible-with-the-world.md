---
title: Is pure goodness incompatible with the world?
---

# Definition

Raised reading *The Idiot*: Prince Myshkin doesn't self-mythologize the way the pawnbroker or the Ridiculous Man do, yet his goodness still produces catastrophe (Nastasya's death, Rogozhin's ruin). Is Dostoevsky arguing that pure goodness is simply incompatible with the world, independent of any hidden vanity? Sharpened further in discussion: maybe Myshkin's goodness is suspect not for vanity but for being too frictionless — too close to the "crystal palace" rationality that *Notes from Underground* rejects. He never says "I don't think so" to anything, which may be exactly why society calls him an idiot. There's a comparison to Sade's *Justine* worth keeping: in Sade virtue is presented as illusion, but Dostoevsky's question feels crueler — the virtue is real, the question is only whether it's *enough*.

A second layer, worked out later: Myshkin's compassion toward Nastasya may itself be a quiet form of superiority. Seeing someone as a victim who needs saving isn't the same as seeing them as a full person — his compassion never really asks whether she asked to be saved, and that gap between pity and recognition is where good intentions curdle into a subtler version of the same instinct as the novel's more openly self-interested characters. At the end, he chooses Nastasya over Aglaya not because he wants her more, but because his moral compass points automatically toward whoever seems to be suffering most — meaning he never actually chooses anyone because he desires them, only because he judges them to need him. Trying to answer to everyone's suffering at once, he ends up sacrificing the one thing nobody else in the book sacrifices for him: a life of his own.
