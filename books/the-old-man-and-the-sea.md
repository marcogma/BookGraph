# Summary

Santiago, an old Cuban fisherman, has gone eighty-four days without a catch. The boy Manolin, who used to fish with him, has been sent by his parents to a luckier boat but still looks after him. On the eighty-fifth day Santiago rows far out into the Gulf Stream and hooks a huge marlin, which pulls his skiff out to sea for two days and nights. His hands bleed and cramp, he talks to himself and to the fish, which he calls his brother, and remembers his youth, lions on an African beach, and a hand-wrestling match that lasted a whole night. On the third day he harpoons the marlin and lashes it to the side of the boat, since it is longer than the skiff. On the way back, sharks attack, drawn by the blood. He kills several with the harpoon, then a knife tied to an oar, then a club, but they eat all the meat. He reaches the harbor at night with only the skeleton and sleeps in his shack. Fishermen measure the skeleton, eighteen feet long. Manolin cries when he sees his hands and says he will fish with him again. Santiago sleeps and dreams of the lions.

# Main characters

- **Santiago**: the old fisherman
- **Manolin**: the boy who loves him
- **The marlin**: the great fish he fights for three days
- **Joe DiMaggio**: the baseball player Santiago admires, whose father was a fisherman

# Historical & cultural context

Published in 1952 in a single issue of *Life* magazine, which sold over five million copies in two days. It won the Pulitzer Prize in 1953 and was cited when Hemingway (1899–1961) won the Nobel Prize in 1954. Hemingway lived in Cuba from 1939 to 1960, at Finca Vigía near Havana, and kept his fishing boat, the *Pilar*, at Cojímar, the village the story is based on. Gregorio Fuentes, his boat's captain, is often named as one model for Santiago.
