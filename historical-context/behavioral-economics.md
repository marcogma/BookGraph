# What happened

Standard economics assumed people make rational, consistent choices. In the 1970s the Israeli psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky ran simple experiments showing that people rely on shortcuts (heuristics) that produce predictable errors. Their 1979 paper on prospect theory showed that people weigh losses more heavily than equal gains. The economist Richard Thaler, who worked with them, applied these findings to economics: why people overvalue what they own, how they keep money in separate "mental accounts", why they fail to save.

Tversky died in 1996. Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002 and died in 2024; Thaler won it in 2017. *Nudge* (2008), by Thaler and the law professor Cass Sunstein, turned the research into policy advice. In 2010 the British government set up a Behavioural Insights Team, nicknamed the "Nudge Unit", and Sunstein ran the White House regulatory office under Obama. During the replication crisis of the 2010s some well-known findings, especially on priming, failed to replicate; in 2012 Kahneman wrote an open letter to priming researchers asking them to address it.
