# What happened

Around 1879, already the internationally famous author of *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*, Tolstoy underwent a severe spiritual crisis — documented in *A Confession* (written 1879–80, published 1882) — in which he came to see his wealth, fame, and even his own earlier novels as morally empty, and arrived at a radical, ascetic reading of Christianity centered on nonviolence, simplicity, and direct moral duty to the poor. He tried (largely blocked by his family) to renounce his property and copyrights, dressed and worked as a peasant, and increasingly wrote short, deliberately plain moral fiction aimed at ordinary and peasant readers rather than the literary novels that had made him famous — a body of work now often called his "Tolstoyan" or post-conversion period.

# Grounds these books

- [[the-death-of-ivan-ilyich]] (1886) — the clearest direct fictionalization of the crisis itself: a man discovering, only in the face of death, that his entire socially-approved life was never "the real thing"
- [[how-much-land-does-a-man-need]] (1886) — same year as *Ivan Ilyich*, but written in the plain-fable register this period favored, aimed at a peasant readership rather than a literary one
- [[master-and-man]] (1895) — written near the end of this period; Vasili Andreich's death saving Nikita dramatizes in narrative form exactly the ethic — sacrifice, plain honesty, dying well — that *A Confession* argues for directly
