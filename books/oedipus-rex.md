# Summary

Thebes is struck by plague. King Oedipus, who saved the city years before by solving the Sphinx's riddle and married the widowed queen Jocasta, sends his brother-in-law Creon to the oracle at Delphi. The answer: the plague will end when the murderer of the previous king, Laius, is found and expelled. Oedipus swears to find him. The blind prophet Tiresias says Oedipus is the murderer; Oedipus accuses him and Creon of plotting against him. Jocasta tries to reassure him: an oracle once said Laius would be killed by his own son, but their baby was left to die on a mountain and Laius was killed by strangers where three roads meet. Oedipus remembers killing a man at such a crossroads. A messenger from Corinth brings news that King Polybus, whom Oedipus thinks is his father, has died, and reveals that Oedipus was adopted, a baby handed to him by a shepherd of Laius. The shepherd confirms it. Oedipus has killed his father and married his mother. Jocasta hangs herself. Oedipus blinds himself with the pins of her brooch and asks to be exiled.

# Main characters

- **Oedipus**: king of Thebes, who investigates himself
- **Jocasta**: his wife and mother
- **Creon**: Jocasta's brother
- **Tiresias**: the blind prophet
- **The messenger from Corinth**: who reveals Oedipus was adopted
- **The shepherd**: who saved the baby instead of leaving him to die
- **The chorus**: the elders of Thebes

# Historical & cultural context

See [[fifth-century-athens]]. Aristotle, in the *Poetics*, used this play as his model tragedy, especially for its reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis) happening at the same moment. Freud took the name "Oedipus complex" from it in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1899).
