# Summary

Gilgamesh, the powerful king of Uruk, part god and part man, oppresses his people. The gods create Enkidu, a wild man who lives with animals, to be his match. The temple woman Shamhat civilizes Enkidu; he goes to Uruk, wrestles Gilgamesh, and they become close friends. Together they travel to the Cedar Forest and kill its guardian, Humbaba. When the goddess Ishtar proposes to Gilgamesh and he insults her, she sends the Bull of Heaven, and they kill it too. The gods decide that Enkidu must die for this, and he falls sick and dies. Gilgamesh, terrified of death, wanders to find Utnapishtim, the one man the gods made immortal. Utnapishtim tells him the story of the flood: the god Ea warned him to build a boat and save his family and animals. Gilgamesh fails a test to stay awake for seven days. Utnapishtim tells him of a plant that restores youth; Gilgamesh gets it from the bottom of the sea, but a snake steals it while he bathes. He returns to Uruk and shows the ferryman its great walls.

# Main characters

- **Gilgamesh**: king of Uruk
- **Enkidu**: the wild man who becomes his friend
- **Shamhat**: the temple woman who brings Enkidu to civilization
- **Humbaba**: guardian of the Cedar Forest
- **Ishtar**: goddess of love and war
- **Utnapishtim**: survivor of the flood, granted immortality
- **Urshanabi**: the ferryman who takes Gilgamesh to Utnapishtim

# Historical & cultural context

See [[homeric-epic-and-oral-tradition]] for comparisons with Homer. Gilgamesh was probably a real king of Uruk, around 2700 BCE. Sumerian poems about him date from about 2100 BCE; the standard Akkadian version on twelve clay tablets was compiled around 1200 BCE. The tablets were found in 1849–1853 in the ruins of the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. In 1872 George Smith of the British Museum read the flood tablet and announced a Babylonian story close to Noah's flood in Genesis.
