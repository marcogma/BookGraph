# Summary

*Brave New World* (1932): in the year 632 "After Ford", the World State produces humans in hatcheries and conditions them from birth into five castes, from Alphas to Epsilons. Everyone is kept content with consumer goods, casual sex, and the drug soma, and family, art, religion and solitude have disappeared. Bernard Marx, an unhappy Alpha, takes Lenina Crowne on holiday to a "savage reservation" in New Mexico. There they meet Linda, a woman from the World State who got pregnant and was left behind, and her son John, raised on Shakespeare and Indigenous rituals. Bernard brings them to London to humiliate the Director of Hatcheries, John's father. John becomes a celebrity, "the Savage". He is attracted to Lenina but disgusted by her casual approach to sex. After Linda dies of soma overdose, he tries to throw away the workers' soma and starts a riot. The World Controller Mustapha Mond explains that stability requires giving up truth, beauty and freedom. John demands the right to be unhappy. He withdraws to a lighthouse to live alone and whip himself, crowds of tourists come to watch, and after a night of orgy he hangs himself. *Brave New World Revisited* (1958) is a set of essays in which Huxley asks how close the world has come to his novel: overpopulation, propaganda, brainwashing, subliminal advertising, drugs, and the rise of bureaucratic power.

# Main characters

- **Bernard Marx**: an Alpha who doesn't fit in
- **Lenina Crowne**: a popular worker at the hatchery
- **Helmholtz Watson**: Bernard's friend, a writer of slogans who wants to write something real
- **John (the Savage)**: born on the reservation, raised on Shakespeare
- **Linda**: John's mother
- **Mustapha Mond**: one of the ten World Controllers
- **The Director (Thomakin)**: head of the hatchery, John's father

# Historical & cultural context

Huxley (1894–1963) came from a family of scientists: his grandfather Thomas Henry Huxley was "Darwin's bulldog" and his brother Julian was a biologist who promoted eugenics. The novel reacts to Henry Ford's assembly line and to American mass culture Huxley saw on a 1926 trip. The title comes from Miranda's line in Shakespeare's *The Tempest*. He later wrote *The Doors of Perception* (1954) on his experience with mescaline. Compare *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, whose author was briefly Huxley's French student at Eton.
