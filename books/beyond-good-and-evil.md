# Notes

Nietzsche disagrees with Kant's categorical imperative because he sees it as herd morality dressed up as universal reason — a demand that moral principles apply to everyone regardless of circumstance, when Nietzsche thinks morality should come from strong, creative individuals, not a one-size-fits-all rule that stifles greatness. He goes further and reads Kant's whole framework as a refined version of Christian slave morality: universal duty ends up suppressing the instincts of the strong in favor of what benefits the herd, and that makes it life-denying rather than life-affirming.

He makes a similar move against the Stoics, accusing them of self-deception — they claim to live according to nature (the *logos*), but Nietzsche argues they're actually imposing their own moral ideal onto a nature that is chaotic, indifferent, and driven by power struggles, not reason. In his reading, the Stoics act like rulers over nature while pretending to be its humble servants.

I pushed back on this one. Why would self-control or the pursuit of virtue actually limit someone's real potential? Nietzsche treats self-control as suppression, repressing instinct in favor of a rigid rational order. But I think that's not the only way to read self-control — it can also be mastery rather than suppression: someone who lets fear or anger control them becomes a slave to those impulses, so real discipline is what frees you to act deliberately instead of reactively. Great artists, athletes, and thinkers use discipline to maximize their potential, not shrink it. Where Nietzsche sees Stoic self-mastery as detachment and acceptance, and his own self-mastery as active, creative, and life-affirming — embracing weakness and instinct rather than overcoming them, transforming suffering into strength instead of denying it exists — I'm not convinced the Stoic version is really the passive one it gets painted as.

Early in the book he criticizes philosophers who assume truth must originate from some higher, self-generating source — I connected this to Descartes' *cogito*, since Descartes searches for an undeniable foundation and ends up needing to prove God's existence to secure it. Nietzsche would read that as smuggling in exactly the kind of metaphysical assumption he rejects: that truth needs a divine or absolute anchor at all, rather than being a human, historical, perspectival construction. The same critique extends to Plato and Kant.

He also brings up Spinoza, whom I hadn't read yet at the time — Nietzsche respects Spinoza's rejection of traditional morality but criticizes his belief in rational necessity and determinism, since Nietzsche sees reality as chaotic and full of creative and destructive power struggles rather than logically necessary.

Later, in the section on skeptics (chapter 6, "We Scholars," section 208), I got stuck on why Nietzsche treats skepticism as a disease, since to me skepticism just means not believing everything at first and looking for a rational explanation. Working through it: he's not attacking healthy doubt used as a method by a strong mind to test its own beliefs. He's attacking skepticism as an end in itself — a symptom of exhaustion, a "degeneracy of the instincts into a desire for peace... for an end to all searching, for a sort of no-longer-being-troubled with either yes or no." That's why he calls Hamlet a fitting prescription for the sick European soul: overthinking to the point of never acting is, for Nietzsche, a failure to affirm life, not a virtue of caution.
